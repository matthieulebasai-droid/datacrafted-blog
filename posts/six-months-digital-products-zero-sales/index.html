6 Months, 15 Tools, 3 Stores, 0 Sales — What I Learned Marketing Digital Products

Six months ago I had a simple theory: build useful developer tools, put them on a few stores, and the sales will follow.

Today I have 15 tools shipped, 3 stores live, a GitHub organization, a brand, a website… and exactly $0 in revenue.

This isn't a humblebrag and it isn't a pitch. It's a transparent post-mortem of what I did, what I got wrong, and what I'd tell my past self — in the hope that someone else skips the expensive parts.

What I actually did

The build phase (months 1–3). I built Python CLI tools for data chores: CSV analysis, JSON→CSV conversion, data cleaning, report generation, plus automation scripts and prompt packs. Each one tested, documented, with a proper README and CI badge.

The store phase (months 3–4). I opened three stores simultaneously:

  • 🟣 Gumroad — 10 products, best API of the three, easy CRUD
  • 🔵 Payhip — better SEO potential, but the API auth was broken for me, so I fell back to browser automation to create products. Noindex by default — invisible to Google until manually disabled
  • 🟡 Lemon Squeezy — clean API, but read-only on the free plan, and the store couldn't process sales until Stripe activation completed

The marketing phase (months 4–6). Reddit posts, GitHub READMEs, a brand site, free "lite" versions of every tool as lead magnets.

The results: 15 tools shipped. ~60 total store visitors (mostly me). 0 sales. 0 email subscribers. 3 stores doing triple the maintenance for the same zero result.

The 7 lessons, in the order I wish I'd learned them

1. Distribution is the product. You can't bolt it on afterward.

I spent 3 months in complete silence — building, polishing, testing. Nobody knew these tools existed, because I never told anyone until the "perfect" launch. The tools that got any attention were the ones I posted about early, rough and unfinished. Feedback is the product; code is just the raw material.

2. "Build it and they will come" is a fairy tale for developers

The most dangerous sentence in a developer's head is "if it's good enough, people will find it." Stores don't give you traffic — you bring the traffic. Gumroad, Payhip, and LemonSqueezy are all dead ends without an audience pointing at them.

3. One store, done well, beats three stores done badly

Three stores meant: three product descriptions to maintain, three different APIs to fight, three different SEO pitfalls (Payhip's noindex nearly killed my Google visibility). All for the same zero sales. The maintenance cost of a channel is part of its price.

4. Free tools are marketing, not products

My free csv-quick-stats got 10x the attention of any paid tool. At first that felt like failure — nobody pays for my work! — then I realized: the free tools are the funnel. They're the lead magnet, the proof of competence, the reason someone trusts the paid versions. I had the funnel backwards: I was hoping paid tools would funnel people to the free ones.

5. Documentation is the real product

Tools with excellent READMEs, worked examples, and screenshots got starred and shared. Tools with "just the code" got ignored. When you sell developer tools, you're selling trust and clarity, not functions. The docs are where the sale happens.

6. Perfectionism is a tax on learning

I spent 6 weeks polishing one tool before release. Nobody cared. The tools I shipped rough got actual feedback, actual issues, actual feature requests — which told me what people wanted better than any roadmap ever could.

7. The $0 question has an uncomfortable answer

I kept asking myself: is $0 marketing even possible in 2026? The honest answer I've landed on: $0 marketing is possible — but only if you substitute time and consistency. One Reddit post does nothing. One post a week, answering every comment, for 3 months, starts to do something. The people who win with organic are boring and relentless, not clever.

What I'd do differently

If I could restart with what I know now:

  1. Pick one store (Gumroad, honestly) and go deep
  2. Build the audience first — write about the process of building the tools, not the tools themselves
  3. Ship the free tool on day one, on GitHub, and let it be the lead magnet
  4. Post weekly to r/Python and r/SideProject — not launch-day, every week
  5. Fix SEO before listing — custom domain, noindex off, one clear landing page
  6. Treat "0 sales" as data, not shame — every zero is a signal about what to change

The uncomfortable conclusion

After 6 months and 0 sales, the most valuable thing I have isn't the stores or even the tools. It's the clarity about what doesn't work — and the small, honest audience that started forming the moment I stopped hiding the numbers and started sharing the journey.

If you're a solo developer about to "launch" a product into the void: build the tool, sure. But spend at least as much time on who will see it as on what it does.
